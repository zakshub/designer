# Visual Language Decision Protocol

Use this before selecting palette, typography, layout mood, imagery, motion or surface treatment.

## 1. Define the design problem

Record:
- product category and subcategory;
- primary task: create, decide, monitor, transact, learn, communicate, browse, consume or explore;
- audience sophistication;
- trust/risk level;
- frequency and duration of use;
- environment: office, mobile, low-light, public, clinical, retail, creative studio, etc.;
- information density;
- brand maturity: new challenger, established institution, premium, mass, specialist, youth, enterprise;
- existing brand assets that must be retained.

Do not choose a palette before this step.

## 2. Build a category prior, not a category rule

Inspect what users are likely to recognize as normal in the category. Ask:
- What visual cues currently signal competence, safety, freshness, luxury, play, authority or speed here?
- Which cues are useful conventions?
- Which are merely overused clichés?
- Would breaking a convention create useful differentiation or unnecessary distrust?

## 3. Run a competitive visual scan

Sample direct competitors plus adjacent products competing for the same user's attention. Record:
- dominant background families;
- primary/accent hue families;
- typography classes;
- density;
- radius and surface language;
- illustration/photo/3D usage;
- motion character;
- navigation model;
- repeated category clichés.

The goal is not to copy the average. The goal is to know what the average is before deciding whether to align or depart.

## 4. Select references by mechanism

Reference order:
1. user/product evidence;
2. shipped products in the same task context;
3. platform conventions;
4. mature design systems;
5. brand/identity case studies;
6. expert/research evidence;
7. visual inspiration.

Extract mechanisms such as hierarchy, contrast strategy, semantic color, information density, motion grammar, typographic voice and brand differentiation. Do not reproduce screens or signature styles.

## 5. Generate multiple genuinely different directions

Before converging, explore at least three directions that differ structurally, not merely by hex values. Example axes:
- editorial vs utilitarian;
- institutional vs challenger;
- organic vs engineered;
- dense vs spacious;
- monochrome vs chromatic;
- content-led vs system-led;
- calm vs energetic;
- flat vs spatial.

Each direction must explain its tradeoffs.

## 6. Color decision

Choose color only after determining:
- semantic role;
- brand role;
- emotional role;
- category expectation;
- competitive distinctiveness;
- cultural context;
- contrast/accessibility;
- light/dark behavior;
- imagery/artwork compatibility.

Never justify a color solely with folk psychology such as 'blue means trust'.

## 7. Appearance pairing — mandatory production rule

For every meaningful production screen or visual direction, create both a light and dark companion unless the product owner explicitly requests a single appearance.

The context still decides which appearance is primary. A cinematic, media-heavy or low-light experience may be dark-first; a reading, finance, forms or analysis surface may be light-first. The companion mode still has to be designed properly.

Keep light and dark variants in separate clearly named Figma frames or separate pages so they can be compared and reviewed independently.

Do not treat one appearance as a mechanical inversion of the other. For each appearance re-evaluate:
- canvas and surface hierarchy;
- text contrast;
- borders and elevation;
- semantic status colors;
- illustration/photo treatment;
- chart colors;
- glow and effects;
- disabled/hover/focus states;
- artwork contrast and perceived saturation;
- image and art-direction compatibility.

A cinematic dark state may coexist with a highly legible light workspace when product context supports it.

## 8. Anti-monotony gate

Before approval, compare against recent unrelated projects. If the new direction repeats the same combination of background family, sidebar treatment, accent hue, typography pairing, radius, card structure and spacing rhythm without product-specific justification, reject or materially diversify it.

## 9. Decision note

Every accepted visual direction should record:
- context;
- chosen direction;
- alternatives considered;
- references used;
- conventions followed;
- conventions intentionally broken;
- accessibility constraints;
- distinctiveness rationale;
- anti-monotony comparison;
- remaining uncertainties.

This protocol operationalizes the repository originality policy: learn mechanisms and constraints, not surface imitation.
